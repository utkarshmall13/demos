Model. Plain linear logistic regression, P(y = 1 | x) = σ(wTx + b), fitted by minimising the logistic loss plus (λ/2)‖w‖² (the intercept b is not penalised) with Newton's method. No feature expansion is used. Why λ trades bias for variance even for a linear model. With no penalty the fit is the maximum-likelihood boundary, which for Gaussian classes tracks the Bayes direction Σ−1(μ₁ − μ₀) but is noisy when N is small, when labels are flipped, or when far-away outliers dominate the loss (high variance). As λ grows the direction of w is pulled towards the plain mean difference μ₁ − μ₀ (the nearest-centroid rule), and finally w → 0 so that every point gets the majority class (high bias). When the shared covariance is elongated and not aligned with μ₁ − μ₀ (the ESL §4.3 / Friedman 1989 setting) those two directions differ a lot, so the bias grows early; for spherical classes (Duda & Hart) they coincide and shrinkage is almost free until the collapse. Datasets. Anisotropic shared-covariance Gaussians; isotropic Gaussians; Tukey's contaminated normal (10% of points drawn with 3× the spread, a classic robustness test); and Gaussian class-conditionals fitted to Fisher's Iris (versicolor vs virginica on petal length and width, pooled covariance, published class means, standard deviations and correlations). All have a linear Bayes boundary, drawn in black. Class prior. π₁ is the probability of class 1; the Bayes boundary shifts by log(π₁/π₀). With exactly balanced classes the fully regularised model predicts the majority class of its own sample, a coin flip, so variance rises again at the far end of the chart; π₁ = 0.65 makes that collapse deterministic, leaving pure bias. A linear model in 2D has only three parameters, so its variance is inherently small: expect the under-regularised end to sit only modestly above the minimum, and the over-regularised end far above it. How this is computed. M independent training sets of N points are drawn (optionally with a fraction of training labels flipped). For each λ on a log grid the fitted model's hard prediction fD(x) is evaluated on a uniform 80×80 grid. Writing p(x) for the fraction of training sets predicting class 1 at x and y(x) ∈ {0, 1} for the Bayes-optimal label, Bias²(x) = (p(x) − y(x))², Variance(x) = p(x)(1 − p(x)), and the expected 0-1 error P[fD(x) ≠ y(x)] equals Bias²(x) + Variance(x) exactly, with zero irreducible error. Averaging over the grid gives the curves. Click or drag on the chart to change λ.
