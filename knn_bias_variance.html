How this is computed. The true labels come from a noise-free function, so the irreducible error is zero. M independent training sets of N points are drawn uniformly from the square and labelled by that function. For every k from 1 to N, each training set's hard k-NN prediction fD(x) is evaluated on a uniform 80×80 grid (only odd k are used, so majority votes never tie). Writing p(x) for the fraction of training sets predicting class 1 at x and y(x) ∈ {0, 1} for the true label, Bias²(x) = (p(x) − y(x))², Variance(x) = p(x)(1 − p(x)), and the expected 0-1 error P[fD(x) ≠ y(x)] equals Bias²(x) + Variance(x) exactly. Averaging over the grid gives the curves. Click or drag on the chart to change k. Small k gives jagged, sample-specific boundaries (high variance); large k gives smooth boundaries that cannot follow the true one (high bias). For k close to N the prediction becomes the majority class of the whole training set; when the classes are balanced that flips between samples, so the variance can rise again at the far right. The optional training-label noise flips that fraction of the training labels; bias and variance are still measured against the clean labels, so the irreducible error stays zero and the identity still holds, but 1-NN now memorises the flipped points and the optimum moves to a larger k. With 0% noise, 1-NN is the best choice for every dataset here: without label noise there is nothing to average out, and a larger neighbourhood only widens the error band along the boundary.
